Reflection 02

Security Threat Landscape and Social Engineering

Brian Justine D. Alcoran BSIT — Information Security

New Insights

Before this discussion, I mostly thought of cybersecurity threats as things like viruses, hacking, or stolen passwords. Learning about social engineering made me realize that attackers can also target the person behind the device. Instead of directly attacking a system, they can manipulate someone into giving information or doing something that puts security at risk. The different techniques we discussed, such as phishing, spear phishing, vishing, smishing, pretexting, and baiting, showed me how many ways an attacker can approach a victim.

What stood out to me the most was that some attacks can happen without using complicated technology. Techniques like shoulder surfing, tailgating, and dumpster diving can happen in normal physical situations. This made me realize that being secure also depends on being aware of what is happening around us.

Concepts I Want to Learn More About

After discussing social engineering, I became interested in learning more about how to identify these attacks before they become a problem. I want to learn more about how security professionals recognize suspicious emails, messages, phone calls, and online accounts. I also want to understand more about how companies train their employees to recognize social engineering attempts.

Another concept I would like to explore is the psychological side of these attacks. The reference explains that attackers can use tactics such as authority, urgency, fear, curiosity, greed, trust, sympathy, scarcity, and reciprocity to influence people. I find this interesting because it shows how an attacker can use someone's emotions to make them act without thinking carefully.

Confusion or Challenges

One challenge I experienced was telling the difference between some of the social engineering techniques because several of them are closely related. For example, phishing, spear phishing, and whaling all involve deceptive messages, but they differ based on the people being targeted and how specific the attack is.

I also had to remember that social engineering is not limited to emails or online messages. Some techniques can involve phone calls, SMS, social media, websites, or even face-to-face situations. At first, I found it a little confusing to remember all the different techniques, but looking at real-life examples made them easier to understand.

Personal Reflection

This discussion made me more careful about the information I share and the messages I receive. Before, I might have focused more on whether something looked suspicious technically, but now I also think about the reason behind the request and whether someone is trying to pressure or manipulate me.

As a BSIT student, I think this topic is important because I will probably encounter different security threats in the future. Knowing about social engineering can help me become more careful not only with my own accounts but also when working with other people and handling information. This lesson reminded me that security is not only about technology; it also depends on awareness and the decisions people make.
